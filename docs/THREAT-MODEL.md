# Nullecho — threat model

The point of this document is to make overclaiming impossible. Every user-facing
sentence gets checked against it. A privacy tool that implies more protection than it
delivers causes real harm — people make exposure decisions based on what we imply.

---

## THE CANONICAL CLAIM

This is the strongest thing Nullecho is allowed to say about itself. Every piece of
user-facing copy — popup, options, store listing, site, README — must be consistent
with it, and nothing may claim more. Adopted after measuring the shim against the
Arkenfox critique; see `docs/ARKENFOX-RESPONSE.md` and `DECISIONS.md` D11.

> **Nullecho shows each site a different, internally consistent device profile, which
> breaks the device-fingerprint join that ad-tech uses to follow you between sites. It
> is detectable, it does not defeat a determined adversary, and it does nothing about
> your IP address.**

Every clause is load-bearing and every one is backed by a measurement:

| Clause | Evidence |
|---|---|
| "different … per site" | `personaFor(salt, eTLD+1)` — measured: 5 sites, 4 distinct personas. |
| "internally consistent" | 39/39 consistency assertions in `harness/shim-test.html`. |
| "breaks the join" | Composite `0c5e721f1ea5ac` → `1e2351e8d63403`, 8 of 14 signals changed, stable across runs. |
| "it is detectable" | 2 of 9 adversarial detectors fire, and we say so rather than hiding it. |
| "does not defeat a determined adversary" | Engine-level invariants (libm, rasterizer, display via CSS `@media`) survive every persona. |
| "nothing about your IP" | Unreachable from a WebExtension. |

### Say this too, in-product

Nullecho must tell users when something else is genuinely better:

> **Firefox's `resistFingerprinting` / `fingerprintingProtection`, and Brave's built-in
> protections, are stronger than Nullecho or any other extension** — they operate below
> the JavaScript layer, where an extension cannot reach. Nullecho exists for Chrome,
> where neither is available, and takes a different trade: a different profile per site
> rather than one identical profile for everyone.

That is not modesty for its own sake. The audience most likely to evaluate this tool is
the audience most likely to already know it is true, and a privacy tool that pretends to
be the best option is not trustworthy about anything else.

### Banned phrases

Never ship, anywhere: **"protects you from fingerprinting"**, "prevents fingerprinting",
"blocks fingerprinting", "makes you anonymous", "untraceable", "invisible", "makes you
look like a different computer", or any phrasing implying a site cannot tell Nullecho is
installed. It can, in about ten lines of JavaScript.

## Who Nullecho defends against

**1. Cross-site behavioural profiling (primary).**
Ad networks and data brokers correlating your activity across unrelated sites to build an
interest/identity profile. Mechanisms: third-party cookies, tracking pixels, device
fingerprinting, identifier sync.
→ *Nullecho is effective here.* Blocking cuts collection at source; per-origin personas break
the cross-site join.

**2. Stateless re-identification (primary).**
Being recognised after clearing cookies, in incognito, or across browser profiles, purely
from device characteristics.
→ *Nullecho is effective here, with caveats* (see gaps).

**3. Data brokers holding an existing profile (secondary, US-CA only).**
Data already collected and sold.
→ *Nullecho helps only by routing you to California's DROP*, which forces registered brokers to
delete. This is the one feature that removes data rather than obstructing collection.

## Who Nullecho does NOT defend against

Stated plainly because the original ask for this project was framed around government
overreach, and honesty here matters more than the pitch.

**State surveillance.** Bulk metadata collection, telecom records, licence-plate readers,
biometric databases, lawful-intercept, financial surveillance. None of this runs on
ad-tracking cookies, so degrading ad profiles does nothing to it. That threat model is
addressed by end-to-end encryption, Tor, data minimisation, and legal/political action.
**Nullecho must never imply otherwise.**

**Network-level observation.** Your ISP, a hostile Wi-Fi operator, or anyone watching traffic
sees the sites you visit regardless of what Nullecho does in the page. That needs a VPN or Tor.

**Targeted attack.** If someone specifically wants to identify *you* and has resources, an
extension in a normal browser will not stop them. Nullecho raises the cost of mass, automated,
commercial profiling. That is a different thing from anonymity.

**A logged-in account.** When signed into Google/Meta/anything, you are identified by the
login. No amount of fingerprint randomisation changes that. Nullecho limits what *other* sites
learn — not what a service you're logged into knows about you.

## Known gaps, technical

| Gap | Why | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| **TLS / JA3-JA4 fingerprint** | Generated by the TLS stack before any page JS exists. No WebExtension API reaches it. | None available in-browser. Needs a custom build (Tor/Brave) or proxy. |
| **IP address** | Not visible to or controllable from an extension. | VPN/Tor. Out of scope. |
| **WASM-compiled fingerprinting** | "The WASM Cloak" (2025) shows fingerprinting logic in WebAssembly can re-derive signals without touching the JS APIs we patch. | Partial. Affects every shim-based defense, ours included. Blocking known fingerprinting vendors at the network layer helps; it is not a full answer. |
| **MAIN-world injection race** | Chrome bug: MAIN-world scripts can land after `DOMContentLoaded`, losing the race to page scripts that fingerprint on load. | Static manifest declaration (earliest hook) + a test asserting the shim wins. **Fail loud, never silent.** Note this race also decides whether the handshake nonce below is a secret — see the next row. |
| **`about:blank` / `srcdoc` subframes** | A parent document is same-origin with its own blank child, so it can reach into that realm directly — before, during and after our content scripts run there. The handshake nonce for such a frame is therefore never private, and neither is anything else in it. | None available. This is what same-origin means, not a bug in the handshake. Nullecho does inject into these frames (`match_origin_as_fallback` / `match_about_blank`), so they get a coherent persona rather than the real machine by default — but a parent that wants the real values from its own blank child can have them. The loader deliberately does **not** raise the `nonce-exposed` warning for subframes: it would fire on nearly every page with an ad iframe, and a warning that always fires is a warning nobody reads. |
| **A page that wins the `document_start` race owns the realm** | If page script runs before `src/shim.js`, it can replace `JSON.parse`, `CustomEvent.prototype.detail`, `crypto.getRandomValues` and `addEventListener` before the shim captures them — and then read the handshake nonce out of a legitimate delivery. No in-page defense can recover trust from that position. | The shim captures those primitives as its first act, so beating the handshake requires beating the race — nothing less. `shim-loader.js` **measures** it: from the ISOLATED world, which the page cannot reach, it checks whether any page script had executed when the boot event arrived and reports `nonce-exposed` if one had. Not fixed; made visible. |
| **Behavioural fingerprinting** | Typing cadence, mouse movement, scroll rhythm. Not addressed at all in v0.1. | Out of scope; disclose. |
| **First-party tracking** | A site you actually use can track you on its own site. Always could. | Not a goal — see below. |
| **The display layer** | CSS `@media` mirrors screen geometry, `devicePixelRatio`, colour depth, HDR and gamut, and is evaluated in the style engine below JS. Measured: 9 of 12 display features disagreed with a spoofed `matchMedia`, and cross-origin stylesheets cannot even be read. | **None — we stopped spoofing it.** Screen size, DPR and colour depth now leak truthfully, because a contradiction is worse than a leak (D2). Largest single protection reduction in the shim. |
| **Which OS you run** | **Deliberate since 2026-08-21 (D12).** The persona now always names the host's own OS family. Hiding the OS never worked anyway — the engine leaks it through the rasterizer, the WebGL extension set and the (truthful) display layer — and claiming a foreign one produced a contradiction any page could read in five lines. | **None, by choice.** Sites learn you are on a Mac / Windows / Linux box. They already did. This costs a couple of bits of anonymity-set size and costs *nothing* against the cross-site join, which is broken by the persona differing per origin, not by its being foreign. |
| **Font enumeration beyond the metric APIs** | `offsetWidth`/`offsetHeight`/`measureText`/`getBoundingClientRect` are covered and agree. `getClientRects()` returns a non-constructible `DOMRectList`; SVG text metrics are unpatched. | Partial. Arkenfox's "too many methods" claim is correct here. |
| **Web Workers** | Content scripts do not run in worker scopes. A tracker fingerprinting from `new Worker()` sees the real machine. | None available. |
| **Same-tick child realms** | `window[0]` on an iframe appended in the same synchronous block reaches a pristine realm before any observer fires. | `contentWindow`/`contentDocument` are hooked and a MutationObserver covers the async case. The same-tick case is unreachable. |

## The honest counterweight: our own protections can get users treated worse

This is the finding a privacy tool least wants to publish, so it goes here first. Blocking detection
scripts and rotating identifiers are, to a fraud/bot-detection vendor, **risk signals in their own
right** — documented in the vendors' own product docs (researched 2026-08-21):

- **Fingerprint.com** publishes numeric weights: "Privacy-Focused Settings" (Firefox/Brave-style) =
  **6**, Tor exit = **14** — twice the penalty of being flagged a bad bot. It also sells a "Device
  Rarity" signal that flags `p99.9+` configurations *"even when they are internally consistent."*
- **Sift** counts *"the lack of fingerprint as a factor in its machine-learning models."*
- **Castle** returns `missing_device_data` (*"an attacker attempting to bypass the client-side
  analysis"*) and `new_os`/`new_language`/`new_device` — meaning **any rotation we perform trips a
  named signal.**
- **Arkose** publishes `Hardware_Signals_Suppressed`; **Stripe Radar** and **MaxMind** score
  `is_anonymous_ip`/`ANONYMOUS_IP` as risk-raising.
- Causal proof (Web Runner 2049, DIMVA 2020): changing *only* the IP moved a request Allow →
  Challenge → Block. And FP-Scanner (USENIX 2018) detected **all seven** anti-fingerprinting
  countermeasures it tested with accuracy 1.0 — *"none succeed in lying consistently without being
  detected."*

**What this means for the product, stated plainly:**
- A user who blocks and spoofs may see **more CAPTCHAs and more false declines**. Global false-decline
  rate is ~1.5% of ecommerce; none of it is *attributed* to privacy tools in published data, but the
  mechanism is documented.
- This is *why* D2's per-origin **stable, internally-consistent** persona matters: rarity and
  inconsistency are the scored signals, so an unstable or contradictory persona is worse than none.
  Our design is the mitigation, not an escape.
- **Balance, also honest:** Gulyás et al. (WPES 2018, n=16,393 real users) measured that removing the
  four mainstream blockers dropped uniqueness only ~3.6pp, and concluded **"privacy extensions should
  be considered more useful than harmful."** Gómez-Boix (WWW 2018, 2M fingerprints) found real-world
  uniqueness is **33.6%, not the 83.6%** the privacy-interested cohort shows. The "randomizing makes
  you unique" warning is real for *small-userbase* tools; a widely-used, crowd-shaped tool is net
  positive. Nullecho only earns that by being crowd-shaped (D3) — which is a reason adoption is a
  feature, not vanity.

## An explicit non-goal: unlinkability to a site you're using

Nullecho does *not* try to make you unrecognisable to a site you're actively browsing. That site
can set a cookie; fighting it is unwinnable and breaks functionality for nothing.

The goal is breaking the join *between* sites. This is why personas are stable per-origin:
stability costs no privacy against the threat we actually care about, and buys large gains in
compatibility and in not looking anomalous.

## How we'd know it works

Not by assertion. By measurement, against `research/BASELINE.md`:

1. Composite fingerprint **changes** with the extension enabled.
2. Composite **stays stable** across reloads in the same origin+session (instability is itself a
   fingerprint, and enables averaging attacks).
3. Composite **differs between origins** (this is the actual product).
4. Every persona field remains **internally consistent** — no contradictions a fraud vendor
   would flag.
5. Repeated-read stability test reports STABLE, not per-read noise.
6. Real sites still work: a breakage suite over common flows (login, checkout, maps, video,
   canvas-based charts).

Any claim in the UI that isn't backed by one of these tests does not ship.

## Copy rules

- Never use "anonymous", "untraceable", "invisible", or "protects you from surveillance".
- Never imply protection against governments, ISPs, or law enforcement.
- Prefer concrete counts over scores: "312 trackers blocked on this site" beats "Privacy: A+".
- If a number is an estimate, label it an estimate.
- Frame as privacy noise and data minimisation, never as sabotage or attacking the ad
  industry — that framing is both inaccurate and legally unhelpful.
