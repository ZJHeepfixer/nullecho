Press kit

Nullecho, for reporters and writers.

Nullecho is a free, open-source browser extension for Chrome. It blocks known trackers (ad networks, analytics, social pixels and fingerprinting companies), sends Global Privacy Control, a "do not sell or share my data" signal that California, Colorado and several other states legally recognize for their residents, and shows each website a different, realistic device profile, so most trackers can't link your visits from one site to the next. It needs no account, shows no ads and doesn't send your data anywhere. It was built by Jason Luker, a bar owner and former auto technician in Ridgecrest, California.

Everything here is free to use in coverage of Nullecho. The logos, cards and screenshots below are plain files in this site's folder; nothing on this page records your visit.

Fact sheet

What it does

  • Sends Global Privacy Control, a "do not sell or share my data" signal that California, Colorado and several other states legally recognize, for their residents.
  • Shows each website a different, realistic device profile, so most trackers can't link your visits. Most, not all: see what it can't do.

Why it exists

Jason Luker spent years in automotive service, repairs and diagnostics, and now owns a small pub in Ridgecrest, California. He has run businesses for most of his life and dealt with customers face to face, and he believes in charging a fair price for something you stand behind.

People are noticing that the price they see online might not be the price their neighbor sees. Some companies use personal data to set a personal price; the FTC calls it surveillance pricing. To him, charging someone more because a computer guessed they'd pay it isn't a fair price, so he did two things.

He read the actual laws. New York has required a pricing disclosure since November 10, 2025. Maryland's ban for large food retailers and third-party food delivery began October 1, 2026. Connecticut's law takes effect July 1, 2027, and New Jersey's grocery ban by August 1, 2027. Reading Maryland's own text, he found that a warning-label section some summaries still describe as law was cut before the bill passed. He also filed a public comment with the FTC.

He built Nullecho, a free tool that makes it harder for companies to follow people around the internet in the first place. It makes tracking harder; it can't promise anyone a price.

“It should be one price for all, not lower or higher prices for some.” Jason Luker

What it can't do

A privacy tool that oversells is worse than none, because people make real decisions based on what it implies. These limits are part of the story; please include the ones that matter to yours.

  • Most trackers, not all. Tested against the FingerprintJS and ClientJS libraries, four device profiles got four different IDs, so those couldn't link the visits. A tracker that detects faked values, throws them out and uses what's left can still link them; the free CreepJS library does exactly that.
  • The canvas protection can be beaten. Since October 14, 2026 the project has documented that a page which draws its canvas about 21 times and keeps each pixel's most common value gets the real canvas back, the same under every profile, in under a second. A page that reads the canvas from a Web Worker gets it in one try. No fix has shipped; it is disclosed in the threat model.
  • It is detectable. A site that looks can tell Nullecho is installed.
  • Nothing about your IP address or TLS fingerprint. No browser extension can reach those. That takes a VPN or Tor.
  • Firefox's built-in protection and Brave are stronger at resisting fingerprinting, because they work below the layer an extension can reach. Nullecho's niche is Chrome, where neither exists.
  • It can't get anyone a better price, and it can't end personalized pricing. It makes tracking harder. The best controlled study found that clearing your tracks gave the worse price more often than the better one.
  • A logged-in account still identifies you to the service you're signed in to.
  • It does nothing against government or ISP surveillance. Different threat model entirely.
  • Global Privacy Control's legal weight depends on where you live, and it covers only the browser profile it's installed in.
  • It's an early release. On Linux, the graphics-card names in the device profiles aren't yet verified on real hardware.

Research and the FTC comment

The Maryland finding

Maryland HB 895 as introduced had a section, Com. Law § 13-322, requiring the sentence "THIS PRICE WAS SET BY AN ALGORITHM OR BY USING YOUR PERSONAL DATA." That section was struck before the bill passed. At least one published law-firm alert, and the AI answers of the major search engines, have described it as law. Maryland's own statute site shows "File Not Found" for it. The write-up, with a three-click check anyone can run: nullecho.org/research/#maryland.

The public FTC comment

Jason filed a comment on the FTC's Proposed Enforcement Policy Statement Regarding Personalized Pricing (File No. P034101). The Commission posted it on September 21, 2026: Comment from Luker, Jason, FTC-2026-1057-1137 on regulations.gov.

Not legal advice: it's written by the developer of a free browser extension, not by a lawyer.
