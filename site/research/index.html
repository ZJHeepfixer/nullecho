Algorithmic pricing disclosure law, read from the statutes.

Which states require a personalized-pricing disclosure, the exact words, what is in force, and what is not law. Every claim below is tied to enacted text you can open yourself, and one of them corrects an error that is circulating in client alerts and in the AI answers of the major search engines.

Verified against enacted and codified text as of September 20, 2026. Written by the developer of a free, open-source browser extension, not by a law firm. Nothing here is legal advice; it tells you where the statutes are.

Two states require a sentence. Two more ban a practice.

Commentary often describes three or four states as mandating an algorithmic-pricing disclosure. On the enacted text, two do, and their sentences differ. Two others chose a prohibition instead, with no wording at all.

No conduct is prohibited. Notice must appear on, at or near, and contemporaneous with, every price display it covers. New York's sentence survived a First Amendment challenge in the district court (National Retail Federation v. James, No. 1:25-cv-05500, S.D.N.Y., Oct. 8, 2025). The appeal, 2d Cir. No. 25-2818, is briefed and undecided.

One widely repeated error, and how to check it in three clicks

Maryland HB 895 as introduced carried a second section, Com. Law § 13-322, that would have required every merchant pricing on personal data to display THIS PRICE WAS SET BY AN ALGORITHM OR BY USING YOUR PERSONAL DATA. That section was struck by amendment before the bill passed. It is not law. At least one published law-firm alert describes it as enacted, and so do the AI-generated answers of the major search engines; most firm alerts got it right.

The error is easy to make. The chapter law prints the struck section in strikethrough, and text extraction from the PDF silently drops the strikethrough, so the deleted section reads like enacted text. The State's own codified-statute service settles it:

Run the check yourself. In a browser, not a script: the service renders only with JavaScript.

  1. 1
    mgaleg.maryland.gov/…/StatuteText?article=gcl&section=13-321&enactments=true
    Expect the full text of § 13-321. This is the positive control. If it does not render, stop; the other two results mean nothing.
  2. 2
    …StatuteText?article=gcl&section=13-322&enactments=true
    Expect "File Not Found." There is no § 13-322.
  3. 3
    …StatuteText?article=gcl&section=13-408&enactments=true
    Expect subsection (a) to name "a violation of § 13-321 of this title" and no § 13-322. The codified cross-references were amended for one new section, not two.

Under curl or any non-JavaScript fetch, every section returns "File not Found", including § 13-321. A check without the positive control proves nothing.

Why it matters in practice: a merchant relying on a circulating summary may be building a disclosure for a Maryland section that does not exist while missing the New York section that does.

Presence is not adequacy

The New York Attorney General's letter to Maplebear Inc. (Instacart), dated January 8, 2026, addressed a page that contained the exact statutory sentence and concluded that the disclosure nonetheless failed to be clear and conspicuous, because of where and how it appeared. A compliance program that checks for the presence of a string is checking the wrong thing. The statute's standard is placement and prominence relative to the price.

The same letter is the reason a disclosure rendered as an image, placed inside a collapsed element, or split across markup so the sentence never appears as text should be treated as a risk, not as compliance.

The federal layer

On August 19, 2026 the Federal Trade Commission published a Proposed Enforcement Policy Statement Regarding Personalized Pricing (File No. P034101; docket FTC-2026-1057). Its premise is that a consumer who cannot tell a price was set from their data cannot avoid it, and that concealment is what makes the practice unavoidable. The comment period closes September 25, 2026.

A comment on the Statement was filed from this project on September 20, 2026, making the points on this page: that two states, not three or four, mandate a sentence; that the sentences differ; and that the presence of a sentence should not be mistaken for an adequate disclosure. The Commission posted it on September 21: Comment from Luker, Jason, FTC-2026-1057-1137.

What compliance looks like in the wild

A sweep in August 2026 searched for real-world instances of New York's mandated sentence. It is observation with its coverage limits stated, not a statistical sample. Two findings hold up:

  • One category complies: digital-subscription publishers. The Wall Street Journal, Wired, The New Yorker, the Albany Times Union and NJ.com carry the sentence on renewal pricing, sometimes with a "New York residents: please note…" preamble. Renewal and win-back pricing keyed to an account is textbook personalized algorithmic pricing, and these publishers chose to disclose rather than stop.
  • The disclosures live in renewal email, not on web pages. That matters for anyone measuring compliance from outside, and for any compliance team whose own review looks only at the website.

General retail, in the same sweep, was close to zero.

What Nullecho is, and what it is not

Nullecho is a free, open-source browser extension (MIT license) that blocks trackers, sends the Global Privacy Control signal, and shows each website a different, internally consistent device profile. It collects no data and has no accounts. The pricing research exists because building the tool required reading these statutes closely enough to find that the summaries disagree with the text.

It recognizes New York's sentence

On a page that also carries a machine-readable price, the extension recognizes the mandated sentence and lets the user keep a local, plain-text receipt: the page, the time, the price shown, and the sentence as displayed.

It draws no legal conclusion

It never labels a page "compliant" or "in violation." It records what the page displayed. Whether that display is clear and conspicuous is a question for counsel, and the Instacart letter shows why.

It is measurable

The extension collects nothing, so it cannot report on itself. Instead it lets anyone measure it on their own machine, and its test results are published with the source.
