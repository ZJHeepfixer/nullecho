Your data rights

Ask a company what it knows about you.

In 19 US states (and in Florida, against a handful of very large companies), a privacy law lets you make a company show you the data it holds on you. You can ask for your loyalty-program profile and the conclusions it drew about you, which some states expressly cover. You can also make it delete that data, stop selling or sharing it, and in most of those states correct it. Below is what each right does, which states have it, and four letters you can copy and send yourself.

Nothing on this page is sent anywhere. The letters fill in on your own device. You copy them and send them from your own email or through the company's own privacy-request page. Nullecho never sees, stores or forwards them. There is no form here that submits, no analytics and no cookie.

This is information, not legal advice. Every legal statement links to its source (a statute, a regulation, or a state attorney general's page). We read every source on October 14, 2026. Laws change, so check the linked text before relying on a detail.

What these requests do, and what they don't

They let you see the data a company uses to profile you, delete it, stop it being sold or shared, or fix it when it's wrong. That's all. They are not a way to get lower prices, and nothing here claims they are.

  • Loyalty discounts aren't banned. California's law says it doesn't stop businesses offering "loyalty, rewards, premium features, discounts, or club card programs" consistent with the law Cal. Civ. Code § 1798.125(a)(3), and Virginia's carves out a "bona fide loyalty, rewards … or club card program" Va. Code § 59.1-578(A)(4). Connecticut's and Rhode Island's say the same Conn. Gen. Stat. § 42-520(b) R.I. Gen. Laws § 6-48.1-5(d).
  • California, Virginia and Rhode Island bar punishing you for asking. In California a business may not charge you different prices, including "through the use of discounts or other benefits", because you used these rights § 1798.125(a)(1)(B). Virginia's and Rhode Island's laws have similar rules § 59.1-578(A)(4) § 6-48.1-5(b)–(c).
  • Not every company is covered. Most of these laws apply only above a size threshold. Rhode Island's, for example, starts at companies holding data on 35,000 customers R.I. Gen. Laws § 6-48.1-5(a). Each law also exempts some kinds of data and organizations. A company that says it isn't covered may be right.

Right to know: ask for the profile, not just the file

Every law in the table below gives you a right to access the data a company holds on you (Minnesota's covers the categories of data, a copy of what you gave the company, and a list of the third parties it went to). In California you can ask for the specific pieces of personal information a business collected about you, plus the categories of sources it came from, why it's used, and the categories of third parties it went to Cal. Civ. Code § 1798.110(a).

Inferences: the conclusions a company drew about you

  • California counts "inferences drawn … to create a profile about a consumer" as personal information § 1798.140(v)(1)(K). The Attorney General's formal opinion is that you have the right to know internally generated inferences about you, unless a business shows a statutory exception applies Cal. AG Op. No. 20-303 (2022).
  • Connecticut: since July 1, 2026, the access right expressly includes "any inferences about the consumer derived from such personal data", plus a list of third parties the company sold your data to Conn. Gen. Stat. § 42-518(a) (P.A. 25-113).
  • Oregon defines personal data to include "derived data" ORS 646A.570(13)(a), and lets you get a list of specific third parties your data was disclosed to (the company chooses whether to list those that got yours or those that got anyone's) ORS 646A.574(1)(a).
  • Minnesota lets you get a list of the specific third parties, and where a profiling decision had a legal or similarly significant effect on you, to question it and be told why Minn. Stat. § 325M.14, subd. 1(g)–(h).
  • Other states give a right to access "personal data" without saying whether inferences count. The template asks for them by name anyway. The worst answer is no.

Your loyalty-program profile

Purchase histories are personal information under California's definition § 1798.140(v)(1)(D), and a loyalty account is built from them. It's worth asking for by name because the clearest 2025–26 evidence we found of personal data affecting what people pay is in discounts and offers, not the shelf price:

  • In a June 2026 investigation, Consumer Reports reported that two ride-hail companies say they don't use personal data to set base prices but acknowledge using it for discounts and promotions Consumer Reports, 2026-06-16.
  • In 2025, Consumer Reports obtained one shopper's 62-page grocery loyalty profile through a request under Oregon's privacy law. It labeled him "non-loyal", which CR says likely means fewer of the chain's best discounts, and it had his education and income wrong Consumer Reports, 2025-05-21.

Seeing the profile doesn't change the discounts. It shows you the label, tier or segment you were put in, and gives you something specific to correct or delete.

Right to delete

You can ask a company to delete the data it holds about you. How far that reaches depends on the state:

  • California covers information the business "collected from the consumer", and the business must also tell its service providers, contractors and anyone it sold or shared the data with to delete it, unless that "proves impossible or involves disproportionate effort" Cal. Civ. Code § 1798.105(a), (c)(1). There are exceptions, for example where a business needs the data to comply with a legal obligation § 1798.105(d).
  • Most other states cover data "provided by, or obtained about" you e.g. Va. Code § 59.1-577(A)(3). Oregon spells out that this includes data obtained from another source and derived data ORS 646A.574(1)(c).
  • Iowa and Utah cover only data you provided yourself Iowa Code § 715D.3(1)(b) Utah Code § 13-61-201(2).

Before you delete a loyalty account

Deleting your loyalty data can close the account and lose points or member prices. If you want to know what's in it, send the right-to-know request first and decide after you've read the answer.

Right to opt out of sale and sharing

Every law in the table lets you tell a company to stop selling your data. Most also cover targeted advertising and some kinds of profiling; Iowa's rights list names only the sale of data Iowa Code § 715D.3(1)(d). In California the right covers selling or "sharing" your personal information Cal. Civ. Code § 1798.120(a).

What Global Privacy Control does automatically

Global Privacy Control (GPC) is a signal your browser sends to every site you visit. Nullecho sends it (see our privacy page), and so do some browsers.

  • California: a business that sells or shares personal information must treat a qualifying signal as a valid opt-out "for that browser or device and any consumer profile associated with that browser or device", and, if it knows who you are, for you Cal. Code Regs. tit. 11, § 7025(b), (c)(1).
  • Colorado: the Attorney General lists GPC as a recognized universal opt-out mechanism Colorado AG, Universal Opt-Out.
  • Connecticut and Minnesota also require companies to accept opt-out preference signals for targeted advertising and sales Conn. Gen. Stat. § 42-520 Minn. Stat. § 325M.14, subd. 3.

Its limits: GPC covers only the browser that sends it, so your phone and other browsers need their own. It only matters to companies that sell or share data or use it for targeted ads, and your browser can't see whether a company honored it. A written opt-out (template 3) names you, so it can also cover your account and records that aren't tied to one browser.

In California, you don't have to prove who you are to opt out

"A business shall not require a verifiable consumer request for a request to opt-out of sale/sharing." It may ask only for what it needs to find your records, and must stop selling or sharing no later than 15 business days after it gets the request Cal. Code Regs. tit. 11, § 7026(d), (f)(1). Requests to know, delete or correct are different: the business may verify your identity first Cal. Civ. Code § 1798.140(ak). Use the company's own portal for that, and don't email copies of ID documents.

Right to correct

You can ask a company to correct inaccurate personal information in California Cal. Civ. Code § 1798.106 and in most of the other states below. The exceptions:

  • Iowa: its list of consumer rights has no right to correct Iowa Code § 715D.3(1).
  • Utah: added, in force since July 1, 2026 Utah Code § 13-61-201(4).
  • Indiana: limited to data you provided to the company Ind. Code § 24-15-3-1(b)(2).

Ask for corrections to carry through to any profile or segment built from the wrong data. The template does.

California's 45 days runs from when the business receives your request, even while it verifies who you are Cal. Civ. Code § 1798.130(a)(2)(A); the 10-day confirmation and the 15-day opt-out rule are in the regulations Cal. Code Regs. tit. 11, §§ 7021(a), 7026(f)(1). A business doesn't have to answer a right-to-know request from the same person more than twice in 12 months § 1798.130(b).

Most other states use 45 days plus one 45-day extension; Iowa allows 90 plus 45 Iowa Code § 715D.3(2)(a) and Florida 45 plus 15 Fla. Stat. § 501.706(2). Each state's is in the table. If a company refuses, these laws generally require it to tell you why and how to appeal, for example Va. Code § 59.1-577(B)(2). Keep a copy of what you sent and the date.

California DROP: one request to every registered data broker

The templates on this page go to one company at a time. If you live in California, the state's Delete Request and Opt-out Platform (DROP) sends one deletion request to all registered data brokers. It's free, and it's run by CalPrivacy, the state privacy agency.

  • Status, per CalPrivacy: DROP opened to Californians on January 1, 2026. Since August 1, 2026, data brokers must access it at least once every 45 days to process requests, and status updates can take up to 90 days to appear. CalPrivacy expects every broker to have finished its first cycle by November 2026 privacy.ca.gov/drop.
  • Who can file: California residents; in some cases you can file for another California resident, such as a parent for a child privacy.ca.gov/drop.
  • What it doesn't reach: companies you deal with directly. For those, use the templates.

Four letters you can send yourself

Fill in the blanks once and all four letters update. Then copy one and paste it into an email to the company's privacy address, or into its own request form. The text stays on your device: nothing you type is sent, saved or seen by anyone until you send it yourself.

1. "Tell me what you know about me"

Asks for your data, the inferences and segments drawn from it, and your loyalty profile.

Where to send it: look for "Your Privacy Choices", "Privacy request" or "Do Not Sell or Share" in the company's site footer or privacy policy. A California business must offer at least two ways to send this, usually including a toll-free number § 1798.130(a)(1)(A).

2. "Delete my data"

Asks the company, and the companies it passed your data to, to delete it.

Where to send it: the same privacy-request page or address. Some companies ask you to confirm a deletion request a second time; watch for that email.

3. "Don't sell or share my data"

Opts you out of the sale or sharing of your data, targeted advertising and covered profiling.

Where to send it: the "Do Not Sell or Share My Personal Information" or "Your Privacy Choices" link, usually in the site footer. In California a business that sells or shares must offer that link, unless it honors opt-out signals like GPC instead § 1798.135(a)–(b).

4. "Correct my data"

Asks the company to fix what's wrong, including in profiles built from it.

Where to send it: the same privacy-request page. The company may ask for documents to show the correct value; send them only through its own portal.
