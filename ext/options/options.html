    Pricing notices on shopping pages
    
    
    
      Two U.S. states name an exact sentence a business has to show when it sets
      a price from an algorithm that used your personal data. When Nullecho sees
      one of those sentences on a page that also publishes a price, it tells you
      in the popup. That is the whole feature: it reads what the page displayed.
    
    

    
    
      
    
        
    New York — in force now
    
        
    
          Gen. Bus. Law § 349-a has been enforced since 10 November 2025. Its
          wording is fixed, word for word:
          “THIS PRICE WAS SET BY AN ALGORITHM USING YOUR PERSONAL
          DATA.” A business that personalizes a price from your data has
          to put that sentence on, at or near the price, in the same medium.
          Because the wording cannot vary, it is the one sentence a browser
          extension can look for exactly.
        
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Connecticut — from 1 July 2027
    
        
    
          P.A. 26-130 § 11 names
          “THIS PRICE WAS INCREASED USING YOUR PERSONAL DATA”
          — or a substantially similar disclosure. That last clause
          matters more than it looks: a Connecticut page can say the same thing
          in its own words and Nullecho will not recognise it. So a Connecticut
          match means something; the absence of one means nothing at all.
        
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Maryland and New Jersey chose bans, not sentences
    
        
    
          Both states legislated in 2026, and neither prescribes any wording.
          Maryland’s Com. Law § 13-321 bars dynamic pricing by large food
          retailers and third-party delivery; New Jersey’s Fair Price Protection
          Act bars personalized pricing on groceries. A draft Maryland section
          that would have mandated a sentence was struck by amendment
          before the bill passed, and it is quoted in a good deal of professional
          commentary as though it survived. Nullecho has no branch for it.
        
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Finding the sentence is not a finding about the business
    
        
    
          In January 2026 New York’s Attorney General wrote to Instacart about a
          page that carried the mandated sentence exactly as the statute
          prints it, and said that form of disclosure “does not appear to comply
          with, among other things, the ‘clear and conspicuous’ requirements of
          the Act” — it sat mid-sentence on a policy page reached through fine
          print, and was absent from the pages that actually show prices. So a
          page can carry the words and still be in trouble, and a page with no
          words at all may simply not be personalizing anything. Nullecho reports
          the words. It does not grade anyone.
        
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Why Nullecho watches for this at all
    
        
    
          The Federal Trade Commission’s Proposed Enforcement Policy Statement on
          Personalized Pricing (19 August 2026) describes consumers avoiding
          personalized prices by “using a virtual private network or private
          browsing functionality”. Per-site profiles and tracker blocking are the
          browser-side half of that category. What Nullecho adds is the part
          nobody else does: telling you when the page itself has said out loud
          that a price was personalized.
        
    
      
    
      
    
        
    It will not get you a lower price
    
        
    
          We looked, and the evidence runs the other way. In the largest test of
          it — 372 simultaneous searches, one browser full of tracking data and
          one scrubbed — 88% of fares were identical, and of the pairs that
          differed, 59% had the higher fare on the scrubbed browser.
          Clearing your tracks raised prices more often than it lowered them.
          Nullecho makes no claim about what you pay.
        
    
      
    
    
    

    
    
      The receipt the popup copies, and every observation behind it, stay on this
      machine. Nullecho has no server, no account and no analytics, and a test
      fails the build if any code that could send something is ever added. The
      stored record holds the page address without its query string, the date,
      the price the page published, and up to 200 characters of the text around
      the sentence — nothing about you.
    
    
  
    

  
    
    
    Delete data brokers already hold
    
    
    
      Everything else on this page limits what gets collected from now on. This is
      the only part that removes data that already exists.
    
    
    
    
      California runs a single deletion platform, DROP. Since 1 August 2026 every
      registered data broker must check it every 45 days, delete matching records
      including inferences drawn about you, and report that they did. One
      submission reaches every registered broker.
    
    
    
    
      
    
    
    
    
      Off by default, and it stays on this machine: Nullecho never measures or
      guesses where you are, and nothing about this setting is sent anywhere. It
      only decides whether the popup and this page suggest the California-only
      deletion step. The walkthrough below is always available either way.
    
    

    
    
      Open the deletion walkthrough
    
    
    
    
      Limits, stated plainly: California residents only; the state requires
      identity verification, so Nullecho cannot submit on your behalf and will only
      walk you through it; and it reaches registered data brokers, not
      every company that holds information about you.
    
    
  
    

  
  
    
    
    What Nullecho can’t do
    
    
    
      Kept on the settings page rather than in a footnote, because people make real
      decisions about their exposure based on what a privacy tool implies.
    
    

    
    
      
    
        
    Sites can tell Nullecho is installed
    
        
    About ten lines of JavaScript will do it — filling a canvas with one colour and noticing the pixels are not quite uniform is enough. Nullecho breaks the link between sites; it does not hide that you are using it. Anyone claiming an extension can do that is guessing or selling something.
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Firefox and Brave do this better
    
        
    Firefox's resistFingerprinting and Brave's built-in protections work below the JavaScript layer, where an extension simply cannot reach — so they cover things Nullecho never will. If you can use either, they are the stronger choice. Nullecho exists for Chrome, where neither is available, and makes a different trade: a different device profile per site instead of one identical profile for everyone.
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Your screen size and display
    
        
    Screen dimensions, pixel density and colour depth are reported honestly. CSS style rules can read all of them independently of JavaScript, so faking them produced a profile that contradicted itself — which is worse than the leak, because it is both identifying and looks evasive. A consistent honest answer beats an inconsistent fake.
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Your IP address
    
        
    Every site you visit still sees it, and it is a stable identifier on its own. That needs a VPN or Tor — no browser extension can reach it.
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Your TLS fingerprint
    
        
    The handshake that identifies your browser build happens before any page code runs, including ours. Unreachable from an extension.
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Accounts you are signed in to
    
        
    If you are logged in, you are identified by the login. Changing device details does not change that. Nullecho limits what other sites learn.
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Government or ISP surveillance
    
        
    A different threat model entirely. Bulk metadata collection does not run on ad-tracking cookies, so degrading ad profiles does nothing to it.
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Fingerprinting compiled to WebAssembly
    
        
    Research published in 2025 shows fingerprinting logic in WASM can re-derive some signals without touching the JavaScript APIs we patch. This affects every extension-based defence, ours included.
    
      
    
      
    
        
    How you type and move
    
        
    Typing rhythm, mouse movement and scroll behaviour are all fingerprintable and are not addressed at all in this version.
    
      
    
    
    

    
    
      What Nullecho does do: it cuts tracker requests at the network layer, and it
      makes the device details a site can read differ from site to site, which
      breaks the join most trackers use to follow you between them; a library that detects spoofed values can still re-join.
