California quietly gave you a button that forces data brokers to delete you.

One request, filed once, reaches all 603 data brokers registered in California. They have to delete what they hold on you — including the inferences they made about you — and they have to keep doing it, every 45 days, indefinitely. It's free, it's the state's own system, and it needs three pieces of information.

Open California's DROP portal → consumer.drop.privacy.ca.gov — the state's own portal. This page just explains what to expect.

What changed

California's DELETE Act created DROP — the Delete Request and Opt-out Platform, run by the California Privacy Protection Agency. It opened to consumers on January 1, 2026.

But for the first seven months it was a request going into a queue. That changed on August 1, 2026, when brokers became obligated to actually process what's in it. Most people have no idea it happened.

Why this is different from the usual advice

The standard guidance is to opt out of brokers one at a time — hundreds of separate forms, each with its own process, many of which quietly re-add you. Paid services like DeleteMe and Incogni exist because doing it manually is miserable.

DROP replaces that with one request backed by a statute. And critically, the obligation isn't just to delete what they have today — brokers must keep returning to the queue, so a request filed now also catches data they acquire later.

The part people miss

The deletion obligation covers inferences — the conclusions brokers drew about you, not merely the raw records they bought. That's the part that actually drives what you get shown, what you get offered, and what you get charged.

How to file it

Have these ready before you start. The whole thing is one sitting.

  1. Check whether you qualify

    The test is broader than "lives in California." CalPrivacy's wording covers anyone domiciled in California, including someone outside the state for a temporary or transitory purpose — so students, people on long work assignments, and snowbirds may still qualify. Someone who genuinely moved away does not.

  2. You need three things

    Name, date of birth, and ZIP code. That's the required set. Email, phone, former/maiden names, mobile ad ID, connected-TV ID and VIN are all optional — each one you add widens the net brokers can match you against, so more optional data means more thorough deletion. That trade-off is yours to make.

  3. Submit — no account needed

    Go to consumer.drop.privacy.ca.gov. Residency is verified through the California Identity Gateway, and CalPrivacy states that information is not retained by DROP. You'll get an 8-digit DROP ID — keep it private; it's how you check status later. Don't pay a third-party site to file this for you.

  4. File once. It keeps working.

    This is the part worth understanding: CalPrivacy describes DROP as "ongoing – not a one-time action." There's nothing to renew. Brokers return to the queue at least every 45 days, so a request filed today also reaches data they buy next year. You won't get 603 confirmation emails — silence isn't failure. Status is at the status page.

What this does not do

Worth being straight about, because most coverage of privacy tools isn't.

LimitWhat it means
California only The DELETE Act is state law. Other states may have their own deletion rights — they're generally weaker and filed per-company.
Registered brokers It reaches the 603 brokers registered with California. A company that never registered isn't in the queue — though failing to register is itself a violation.
Filing for others Family situations are provided for — a parent for a child, a family member for an elderly relative. Whether a commercial or professional agent may file on your behalf is not something we could confirm from CalPrivacy's guidance, so we're not claiming it either way.
Not everyone "Data broker" is a legal category. Companies you have a direct relationship with — your bank, your employer, sites you have accounts on — are not brokers and aren't covered.
Not retroactive to ads Deleting broker data doesn't undo profiling by companies you use directly, and doesn't stop new collection going forward.

One caution

You are handing identifying details to a state system in order to have data about you deleted. The CPPA states this information is used only to complete deletion requests and is not sold or shared. That's the trade; it's a reasonable one, but you should make it knowingly.

Not in California?

There's no national equivalent. What's actually worth doing instead:

  • Turn on Global Privacy Control. A browser signal that legally counts as an opt-out in California and several other states. Free, takes seconds, and it's been enforced — a company was fined for ignoring it.
  • Check your own state. A number of states now have consumer privacy laws with deletion rights. They're filed company-by-company, but they're real.
  • Cut collection at the source. Blocking trackers stops new data being created, which no deletion request can do.
